A recommendation report on protecting the learning value of co-op in the time of generative AI

Attached below is my report on how generative AI may be changing the entry-level work that co-op students at Waterloo rely on to build work experience, and recommendations that your office could investigate implementing to make us more prepared for an age of AI.

This report tackles one question. If agentic tools now handle most of the tasks once given to junior engineers, how can ECE make sure co-op remains a place where students learn? To answer this, I conducted my own research and reviewed recent labor market data (finding both positive and negative results) and learning research alongside insights from my own experience and upper-year students who have gone through multiple co-op terms. While the broader impact on entry-level hiring is still actively unfolding, the research consistently shows that students using AI are directly impacted in how they retain and learn knowledge.

To address this risk, the report recommends three ways that could be implemented to protect students’ learning regardless of how the entry-level job market evolves, which are to teach and assess AI use in design courses, add a learning check-in and reflection to co-op work terms, and better track ECE-specific co-op outcomes from employers.

The report is mainly shaped around six published sources, my work experience, and the experiences of upper-year students whose identities are withheld at their request. I would welcome your feedback, and hope for your consideration in implementing measures to address this problem. Thank you for your time.

Sincerely,

Lakshmanan Ramanathan

Section I

I. Executive Summary

Generative AI is changing the work given to junior engineers, though its effect on entry-level hiring is still early and uneven across North America. Its effect on learning is much clearer. In a controlled experiment, programmers who used AI to learn a new coding library scored about 17% lower on a follow-up quiz than those who worked without it, unless they used AI only to ask conceptual questions. Since co-op is where ECE students build their engineering judgment, the core risk is not just fewer co-op roles, but a shallower learning experience in the roles students do get. This report recommends three actions that can protect students’ development regardless of how the job market shifts:

  • Teach and assess responsible AI use in design courses.
  • Add a short learning check-in to co-op work terms, run by the employer.
  • Track ECE-specific co-op outcomes to see how these trends affect Waterloo students.
Section II

II. Recommendations

A. Teach and assess AI use in a design course as a pilot

Pilot a structured AI framework in one second-year ECE design course next term that permits students to use AI freely and completely while requiring them to document and justify why AI was appropriate for specific tasks. Allow repetitive tasks to be done completely by AI if the decision-making process and architecture are understood by the student. Pair this with short no-AI debugging checks and sample oral defenses so students must independently explain and extend their work. Instructors should explicitly train students to ask conceptual questions instead of generating finished code, since controlled experiments show that conceptual questions still preserve learning, while fully passing the task to AI creates severe gaps in comprehension [3]. Every Waterloo course outline already has to state a generative AI policy [7], so this pilot builds on a step instructors already take.

B. Add a short learning check-in to co-op work terms

Partner with Waterloo’s Co-operative and Experiential Education office to introduce a brief midterm check-in structured around expectations set by the student’s supervisor. This would be a check-in that all employers on WaterlooWorks must agree to provide when hiring ECE students, where they assess their students’ technical growth and design decisions on the job. Waterloo already recommends an informal mid-term evaluation [8], so this turns an existing suggestion into a requirement. It will also allow students to further reflect on their understanding of what they learned during the co-op experience. The faculty must also provide employers with a one-page onboarding guide (Appendix B), making it easier for employers to run the check-in without added administrative burden. Since AI increases short-term junior output significantly [4], [5], employers have little immediate incentive to prioritize deep skill retention unless the faculty makes it a condition of hiring from Waterloo’s ECE cohort.

C. Track ECE-specific co-op outcomes

Have ECE faculty staff collect anonymous aggregated data at the end of each work term on placement rates, role responsibilities, and student-reported AI reliance on their work terms, using a few added questions in the Rate My Work Term survey students already complete [9]. Along with this information, review the results of the employer check-ins from Recommendation B to see how well students retain what they learned. Because national labor statistics group occupations broadly [2] instead of isolating engineering disciplines, longitudinal internal tracking will give the department reliable evidence on whether entry-level ECE work and hiring is shifting at Waterloo, as I found with other sources [1], [2].

Section III

III. Introduction

I am Lakshmanan Ramanathan, a first-year Computer Engineering student at the University of Waterloo, and this past summer I worked as a junior backend developer. Notably, during that internship AI was capable of doing everything I did on the job, which hindered my learning experience since my employer preferred using AI as it produced working code much faster. This directly affects me and many of my classmates in ECE, which is why I decided to research this problem.

Engineers become senior by doing and progressing through junior work, where they learn to write first drafts of simple repetitive code, fix small bugs, run different tests and document simple and segmented systems. These tasks are given to students because they are low risk, but they are also how junior engineers learn to read a codebase and make their own design decisions. With the explosive growth of agentic AI tools, which can plan and carry out multi-step coding tasks with very little human input, a large amount of this work is now being done by AI instead of by students. This creates two problems for Waterloo ECE students: fewer entry-level roles and less learning in the roles students do get. This report mainly focuses on the second problem since it affects every student who goes on a work term.

This also matters directly to our Waterloo community, since our students graduate with up to 24 months of paid work experience from four to six co-op terms [10]. Our advantage has always been that we graduate with real experience compared to other students, and that depends on employers giving students real work and on us actually learning on the job.

Section IV

IV. Methods

This project is a literature review supported by student conversations, shaped around the 3 questions from my proposal on hiring, learning, and how Waterloo could respond.

For the hiring question, my strongest source was the August 2026 revision of Stanford’s “Canaries in the Coal Mine?” paper [1], which uses ADP payroll records for millions of U.S. workers and has been updated through June 2026. Since the Stanford data is American, I paired it with Statistics Canada’s October 2026 study on hiring rates [2], which uses Labour Force Survey data from 2006 to August 2026, to test whether the patterns hold true in Canada. I also used the World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025 [6] to see what employers are planning for the future.

For the learning question, I searched arXiv, Google Scholar and the ACM Digital Library through the University of Waterloo Library for controlled studies that test novices after they work with an AI assistant, instead of only measuring how fast they finish. I selected Shen and Tamkin’s randomized experiment with 52 developers [3] and Gardella et al.’s study of 22 novices who were retested one week later [4]. I also used Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond’s study [5] to understand why employers adopt AI for junior staff.

To address the education question, I spoke with four upper-year ECE students who are completing their capstone projects about their co-op experiences, and since they asked to stay anonymous I refer to them as Students A to D. I originally planned to interview professors who teach design courses, but since they were not available in time, I used public course outlines such as ECE 198 [11] along with Waterloo’s course outline requirements [7] and the co-op evaluation process [8], [9] instead. Since Stanford and other sources post updates monthly, I set a data cutoff of November 18, 2026.

Section V

V. Results

A. Entry-level hiring is shrinking

The evidence I gathered on hiring is recent and measurable. Stanford researchers found that the employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations, such as software development, is about 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed occupations, and the gap was 15% just a year earlier [1]. Between November 2022 and June 2026, employment for this group fell about 11% in the most exposed jobs, while it grew about 10% in the least exposed jobs [1]. This drop comes mainly from employers hiring fewer young workers rather than laying them off, and it is concentrated in jobs where AI is used to automate tasks, while experienced workers are not impacted by much [1].

In Canada, Statistics Canada found that youth (15 to 29) hiring rates have fallen in every occupation group, including the jobs least exposed to AI (Fig. 1) [2]. Coding-intensive jobs, which are the closest group to ECE co-op roles, now have the lowest youth hiring rate of all the groups measured at 1.9%, which is a drastic drop from a 15-year high of 5.8% in late 2021 [2]. Although Statistics Canada does not directly blame AI for the overall slowdown yet [2], the result for students is still the same, which is fewer entry-level openings and much more competition for each one. Employers also plan for this to continue, as 40% of employers surveyed by the World Economic Forum plan to reduce their workforce in areas where AI can automate tasks [6].

B. AI increases output but hurts learning

The impacts of AI on learning are much clearer than the hiring data. In Shen and Tamkin’s experiment, developers learned Trio, a Python library for asynchronous programming that none of them had used before, and the group that used AI scored 4.15 points lower out of 27 on the follow-up quiz, which is about 17% lower, while also not being significantly faster [3]. The biggest gap was on debugging questions [3]. Notably, how participants used AI made the biggest difference, since those who passed all of their coding to AI averaged below 40%, while those who only asked conceptual questions and fixed their own errors were in the groups that scored 65% or higher (Table I) [3].

Gardella et al. observed similar findings over a longer period of time [4]. Novices working with GitHub Copilot scored about 14 points higher out of 100 than when they worked with a human partner, and also reported much lower mental demand and effort [4]. However, when they were retested alone one week later, their scores on tasks they learned with Copilot dropped by about 21 points, compared with only about 3 points for tasks they learned with a partner [4]. This shows that AI made the work easier and faster in the moment, but students kept much less of what they did.

This also explains why employers prefer using AI. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond found that across 5,179 customer support agents, an AI assistant increased productivity by 14% on average and by 34% for novice workers, while having very little effect on experienced workers [5].

C. Student and personal experience

The upper-year students I spoke with said that AI helped them speed up coding and syntax fixes on their work terms, but they also said it often hindered their development of foundational debugging skills. They described the bar for entry-level jobs as far greater than what past students had to go through, and there was a lot of negative sentiment about the job search. When I asked what ECE could change, they strongly recommended modernizing the curriculum with industry tools like AI coding CLIs and AI-based work architecture, while also teaching LLM integration.

D. What Waterloo already has in place

Every course outline at Waterloo must already state the course’s policy on generative AI [7]. Employers must also complete a Student Performance Evaluation (SPE) at the end of every work term for the student to receive credit, and they are encouraged but not required to hold an informal mid-term evaluation [8]. Furthermore, students rate every work term anonymously through Rate My Work Term [9], and ECE 198 already teaches first-year students engineering design and project management practices [11].

E. Possible obstacles

The main obstacles to these solutions are the extra time supervisors would need for the check-in, the time instructors would need for oral defenses in large classes, and students who may see no AI debugging checks as a step backward.

Section VI

VI. Discussion

A. Learning is the risk ECE can act on now

The hiring data shows that the entry-level pathway is getting narrower in both the U.S. and Canada [1], [2]. ECE cannot control the job market, but it can control what students learn in the roles they do get, which is where the evidence is the strongest. Both controlled studies show the same pattern, where novices who pass their work to AI perform as well or better in the moment but understand and retain much less afterward [3], [4]. Debugging had the largest gap [3], which is the exact skill the upper-year students said they were losing.

Since a co-op term is four months of this exact type of work, a student could finish a term with a strong evaluation and still not build the engineering judgment the term was supposed to give them. The SPE would not catch this, since it rates performance on the job and AI directly increases performance. This is why Recommendations A and B check understanding directly, and Recommendation C gives the department its own data that national statistics cannot provide [2].

B. Employers will not fix this on their own

Employers have little reason to fix this problem on their own. AI gives the biggest boost to the least experienced workers [5], and 40% of employers are planning to reduce their workforce where AI can automate tasks [6]. This is why the department needs to set the expectation, and Waterloo is in a strong position to do so since over 8,000 employers hire through its co-op program [10].

C. Who is affected

This problem affects students first, but the impact goes further than just our cohort. If students skip the junior stage, they will take a lot longer to become senior engineers, limiting the supply of engineering talent. This also raises an ethical question of who gets opportunities, since students who already have strong fundamentals can use AI to work faster without losing much, while students who are still learning, often in their first or second work term, lose the most. Furthermore, engineers are responsible for the work they approve, so a graduate who cannot debug or explain code written by AI is a safety risk and not just a weaker hire.

D. Alternatives considered

I also considered the option for banning AI in design courses, which would protect learning in the short term, but it goes against what the upper-year students recommended and would leave graduates unprepared for workplaces where these tools are already standard. Another option was leaving the check-in voluntary, which costs nothing, but Waterloo already recommends a mid-term evaluation [8] and that alone has not made it standard.

Section VII

VII. Conclusion

Generative AI is already changing entry-level engineering work. In the U.S., young workers in AI-exposed jobs are being hired much less [1], and in Canada youth hiring has fallen in every occupation group, with coding-intensive jobs now at the lowest rate [2]. In the roles that are left, novices who pass their work to AI finish faster but understand and remember much less, especially when debugging [3], [4], and employers have strong reasons to keep using it [5], [6]. For Waterloo ECE, this means co-op could end up producing work without building the skills students need to become senior engineers. The main takeaways from this report are:

  • The entry-level pathway is getting narrower, and more of the work in the roles that remain is being done by AI.
  • How students use AI matters more than whether they use it, since asking conceptual questions still preserves learning [3].
  • Small changes to processes Waterloo already runs, such as course AI policies, the SPE and Rate My Work Term, can address this problem without building new systems.

I recommend that the department pilot the design course framework next term, work with Co-operative and Experiential Education on the required check-in, and begin tracking co-op outcomes, so that within a year the department has its own evidence on how AI is changing co-op for its students. If this problem is not addressed early, the career ladder could stop producing the engineering talent needed to keep advancing society, and I am eager to help find out what can be done.

Appendix B

Appendix B: Draft Employer Check-In Guide

Purpose. A 20-minute conversation around week six or seven of the work term that checks what the student has learned, in addition to what they have produced.

Questions for the supervisor to ask:

  1. What have you built or changed so far this term?
  2. Which part of that work could you rebuild or debug without AI tools?
  3. Explain one design decision you made and why you chose it.
  4. What skill will you focus on for the rest of the term?

Recording. The supervisor selects one rating in WaterlooWorks (On track, Needs support, or Not yet able to explain own work) and may add one comment. The rating is shared with the student and used only in aggregate by ECE.

Follow-up. If a student is rated Needs support, the supervisor assigns one task in the second half of the term that the student completes and explains without AI.
