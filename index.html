Recommendation Report • University of Waterloo ECE

Protecting the Learning Value of Co-op in the Time of Generative AI

The Core Question: If agentic tools now handle most of the tasks once given to junior engineers, how can ECE make sure co-op remains a place where students learn?

Waterloo students graduate with up to 24 months of paid work experience from four to six co-op terms [10]. Since co-op is where ECE students build their engineering judgment, the core risk is not just fewer co-op roles, but a shallower learning experience in the roles students do get.

Section III • The Career Ladder

How Engineers Become Senior—and How Agentic AI Breaks the Ladder

Engineers become senior by doing and progressing through junior work. With the explosive growth of agentic AI tools—which can plan and carry out multi-step coding tasks with very little human input—a large amount of this work is now being done by AI instead of by students.

Traditional Co-op Pathway

Junior Engineers Do the Low-Risk Foundational Work

These tasks are given to students because they are low risk, and they are how junior engineers learn to read a codebase and make their own design decisions:

1. Write first drafts of simple repetitive code 2. Fix small bugs 3. Run different tests 4. Document simple & segmented systems
Result: Builds deep debugging skills & engineering judgment to become a Senior Engineer.
VS
Agentic AI Co-op Pathway

Agentic AI Automates the Junior Stage

From my own summer internship as a junior backend developer and conversations with 4 upper-year ECE capstone students (Students A–D):

“AI was capable of doing everything I did on the job” “Employer preferred using AI as it produced working code much faster” “Hindered development of foundational debugging skills”
Result: Two problems for Waterloo ECE — (1) Fewer entry-level roles, and (2) Less learning in the roles students do get.
Section V-A • Labor Market Evidence

Entry-Level Hiring is Shrinking Across North America

The evidence on hiring is recent and measurable from Stanford’s August 2026 ADP payroll study [1], Statistics Canada’s October 2026 Labour Force Survey study [2], and the World Economic Forum [6].

Fig. 1. Canadian Youth (15–29) Monthly Hiring Rates [2]

Youth hiring rates have fallen in every occupation group. Coding-intensive jobs (the closest group to ECE co-op roles) now have the lowest youth hiring rate of all groups at 1.9%, a drastic drop from a 15-year high of 5.8% in late 2021 [2].

6 months before Nov. 2022 6 months before Aug. 2026 Coding-intensive (Aug. 2026)
Takeaway: Fewer entry-level openings and much more competition for each co-op role [2].

U.S. Ages 22–25 Employment Divergence (Nov. 2022–June 2026) [1]

Employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations (such as software development) is about 19% below trend (widened from 15% a year earlier) [1].

Why it’s happening: Driven by employers hiring fewer young workers rather than laying them off, while experienced workers are not impacted by much [1]. Furthermore, 40% of employers plan to reduce their workforce where AI can automate tasks [6].
Section V-B • Controlled Learning Experiments

AI Increases Short-Term Output—But Hurts Deep Learning & Debugging

Two controlled studies testing novices after they work with an AI assistant show the exact same pattern: novices who pass their work to AI perform as well or better in the moment, but understand and retain much less afterward [3], [4].

Table I. Quiz Scores by Way of Using AI (Shen & Tamkin, n = 52) [3]

Developers learned Trio (an unfamiliar Python async library). Overall, the AI group scored 4.15 points lower out of 27 (≈17% lower) while not finishing significantly faster [3]. How they used AI made the biggest difference:

Gave all coding to AI (n = 4) below 40%
Asked a few questions, then gave all coding to AI (n = 4) below 40%
Used AI to debug and verify code (n = 4) below 40%
Generated code, then asked follow-up questions (n = 2) 65% or higher
Asked for code with explanations (n = 3) 65% or higher
Asked only conceptual questions & fixed own errors (n = 7) 65% or higher
Key Insight: The biggest gap was on debugging questions [3]—the exact skill upper-year Waterloo ECE students said they were losing.

“Fast and Forgettable”: 1-Week Retest Drop (Gardella et al., n = 22) [4]

Novices working with GitHub Copilot scored ≈ +14 points higher (out of 100) during the initial session with lower mental effort—but when retested alone one week later, their scores plunged by ≈ −21 points [4].

Takeaway: AI made the work easier and faster in the moment, but students kept much less of what they did [4].
Section VI • Discussion

Why Employers—and Waterloo’s Current SPE—Will Not Catch This

Employers have little immediate incentive to prioritize deep skill retention unless the faculty makes it a condition of hiring from Waterloo’s ECE cohort.

Productivity Gain With AI Assistant (5,179 Support Agents) [5]

Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond found that an AI assistant increased productivity by 14% on average and by 34% for novice workers, while having very little effect on experienced workers [5].

The SPE Blind Spot: A student could finish a 4-month co-op term with a strong Student Performance Evaluation (SPE) [8] and still not build engineering judgment, because the SPE rates performance on the job—and AI directly inflates performance.

Who Is Affected & Why Alternatives Fall Short

This problem affects students first, but goes further than just our cohort:

1. Fairness & First/Second Work Terms: Students who already have strong fundamentals can use AI to work faster without losing much, while students still learning (often in their 1st or 2nd work term) lose the most.
2. Engineering Safety Risk: Engineers are responsible for the work they approve, so a graduate who cannot debug or explain code written by AI is a safety risk and not just a weaker hire.
3. Why Not Ban AI or Keep Check-Ins Voluntary? Banning AI in design courses goes against what upper-year students recommended (teaching AI coding CLIs, AI work architecture, and LLM integration) and leaves graduates unprepared. Leaving check-ins voluntary costs nothing, but Waterloo already recommends a mid-term evaluation [8] and that alone has not made it standard.
Section II & Appendix B • Action Plan

Three Recommendations to Protect Student Learning

Small changes to processes Waterloo already runs—course outline AI policies [7], the SPE [8], and Rate My Work Term [9]—can address this problem without building new systems. Over 8,000 employers hire through Waterloo co-op [10], giving the university strong leverage.

A

Teach and assess AI use in a design course as a pilot

  • Pilot a structured AI framework in one second-year ECE design course next term permitting free AI use while requiring students to document and justify why AI was appropriate.
  • Allow repetitive tasks to be done completely by AI if the decision-making process and architecture are understood.
  • Pair with short no-AI debugging checks and sample oral defenses.
  • Explicitly train students to ask conceptual questions instead of generating finished code [3].
Builds on: Mandatory Waterloo course outline AI policy [7] & ECE 198 [11]
B

Add a short learning check-in to co-op work terms

  • Partner with Co-operative and Experiential Education to make a brief midterm check-in required for all employers on WaterlooWorks hiring ECE students.
  • Assesses students’ technical growth and design decisions on the job around week 6 or 7.
  • Provide employers with a one-page onboarding guide (Appendix B) so it adds no administrative burden.
Builds on: Existing informal mid-term evaluation suggestion [8]
C

Track ECE-specific co-op outcomes

  • Have ECE staff collect anonymous aggregated data at the end of each term on placement rates, role responsibilities, and student-reported AI reliance.
  • Review alongside employer check-in ratings from Recommendation B.
  • Gives ECE its own longitudinal evidence within a year, since national statistics group occupations broadly [1], [2].
Builds on: Rate My Work Term survey students already complete [9]
Appendix B • One-Page Employer Onboarding Guide

How the 20-Minute Midterm Check-In Works (Week 6 or 7)

Checks what the student has learned, in addition to what they have produced. The supervisor asks 4 questions:

QUESTION 01 What have you built or changed so far this term?
QUESTION 02 Which part of that work could you rebuild or debug without AI tools?
QUESTION 03 Explain one design decision you made and why you chose it.
QUESTION 04 What skill will you focus on for the rest of the term?
WaterlooWorks Recording & Second-Half Follow-Up Action (Shared with student; used only in aggregate by ECE):
1. On track
Student can independently explain their architecture and debug their work. Continue planned work term goals.
2. Needs support → Action Triggered
Supervisor assigns one task in the second half of the term that the student completes and explains without AI.
3. Not yet able to explain own work
Identifies severe AI over-reliance at week 6–7 so supervisor and student can intervene before the term ends.

Methods & Possible Obstacles (Sections IV & V-E)

Methods: This project is a literature review supported by student conversations, shaped around 3 questions on hiring, learning, and how Waterloo could respond. It pairs Stanford’s August 2026 ADP payroll study [1] with Statistics Canada’s October 2026 Labour Force Survey study [2] and the World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025 [6]; controlled learning experiments by Shen & Tamkin (n = 52) [3] and Gardella et al. (n = 22) [4] alongside Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond [5]; and interviews with four anonymous upper-year ECE capstone students (Students A to D) plus public Waterloo documentation [7]–[11].

Possible Obstacles: The main obstacles to these solutions are the extra time supervisors would need for the check-in, the time instructors would need for oral defenses in large classes, and students who may see no-AI debugging checks as a step backward.
