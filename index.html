Protecting the Learning Value of Co-op in the Time of Generative AI

If agentic tools now handle most of the tasks once given to junior engineers, how can ECE make sure co-op remains a place where students learn?

Since co-op is where ECE students build their engineering judgment, the core risk is not just fewer co-op roles, but a shallower learning experience in the roles students do get.

1. Entry-Level Hiring is Shrinking

Fewer entry-level openings and much more competition for each one

This drop comes mainly from employers hiring fewer young workers rather than laying them off, and it is concentrated in jobs where AI is used to automate tasks, while experienced workers are not impacted by much [1].

Fig. 1. Youth (15 to 29) monthly hiring rates in Canada [2]
Coding-intensive jobs now have the lowest youth hiring rate of all the groups measured at 1.9%, down from a 15-year high of 5.8% in late 2021 [2].

Employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is about 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed occupations (up from 15% a year earlier) [1].
40% of employers surveyed by the World Economic Forum plan to reduce their workforce in areas where AI can automate tasks [6].
2. AI Increases Output but Hurts Learning

How students use AI matters more than whether they use it

Programmers who used AI to learn a new coding library (Trio) scored 4.15 points lower out of 27 (about 17% lower) on a follow-up quiz than those who worked without it, while also not being significantly faster [3]. The biggest gap was on debugging questions [3].

Fast and Forgettable: 1-Week Retest Score Drop [4]
Novices working with GitHub Copilot scored about 14 points higher out of 100 initially than with a human partner, but when retested alone one week later, their scores dropped by about 21 points [4].
This shows that AI made the work easier and faster in the moment, but students kept much less of what they did [4].
3. Why Employers Will Not Fix This on Their Own

AI boosts novice output by 34%—so the SPE will not catch shallow learning

Productivity Gain With an AI Assistant (5,179 Support Agents) [5]
An AI assistant increased productivity by 14% on average and by 34% for novice workers, while having very little effect on experienced workers [5].

Since a co-op term is four months of this exact type of work, a student could finish a term with a strong evaluation and still not build the engineering judgment the term was supposed to give them. The SPE would not catch this, since it rates performance on the job and AI directly increases performance.

Student and Personal Co-op Experience

First-Year Backend Internship: “During that internship AI was capable of doing everything I did on the job, which hindered my learning experience since my employer preferred using AI as it produced working code much faster.”

Upper-Year Capstone Students (Students A–D): “AI helped them speed up coding and syntax fixes on their work terms, but they also said it often hindered their development of foundational debugging skills.”

Safety & Ethics: Engineers are responsible for the work they approve, so a graduate who cannot debug or explain code written by AI is a safety risk and not just a weaker hire.

4. Recommendations

Three small changes to processes Waterloo already runs

Waterloo is in a strong position to set expectations since over 8,000 employers hire through its co-op program [10], and our students graduate with up to 24 months of paid work experience from four to six co-op terms [10].

Recommendation A

Teach and assess AI use in a design course as a pilot

Pilot a structured AI framework in one second-year ECE design course next term that permits students to use AI freely and completely while requiring them to document and justify why AI was appropriate for specific tasks. Pair this with short no-AI debugging checks and sample oral defenses, and train students to ask conceptual questions [3].

Builds on: Mandatory course outline AI policies [7] & ECE 198 [11]
Recommendation B

Add a short learning check-in to co-op work terms

Partner with Waterloo’s Co-operative and Experiential Education office to introduce a brief midterm check-in that all employers on WaterlooWorks must agree to provide when hiring ECE students, where they assess their students’ technical growth and design decisions on the job.

Builds on: Informal mid-term evaluation [8] + 1-page guide (Appendix B)
Recommendation C

Track ECE-specific co-op outcomes

Have ECE faculty staff collect anonymous aggregated data at the end of each work term on placement rates, role responsibilities, and student-reported AI reliance, along with the results of the employer check-ins from Recommendation B.

Builds on: Rate My Work Term survey students already complete [9]
Appendix B: Draft Employer Check-In Guide

Purpose: A 20-minute conversation around week six or seven of the work term that checks what the student has learned, in addition to what they have produced.

  1. What have you built or changed so far this term?
  2. Which part of that work could you rebuild or debug without AI tools?
  3. Explain one design decision you made and why you chose it.
  4. What skill will you focus on for the rest of the term?
Recording: The supervisor selects one rating in WaterlooWorks (On track, Needs support, or Not yet able to explain own work) and may add one comment. The rating is shared with the student and used only in aggregate by ECE.
Follow-up: If a student is rated Needs support, the supervisor assigns one task in the second half of the term that the student completes and explains without AI.
