  • 01 / U.S. Payroll Records [1]

    19% below trend for 22- to 25-year-olds

    Using ADP payroll records for millions of U.S. workers through June 2026, Stanford researchers found that employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations (such as software development) is about 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed occupations—up from a 15% gap a year earlier [1]. Between November 2022 and June 2026, employment for this group fell about 11% in the most exposed jobs while growing about 10% in the least exposed jobs [1].

  • 02 / Canadian Labour Force Survey [2]

    Coding-intensive youth hiring fell to 1.9%

    In Canada, Statistics Canada’s October 2026 study (using Labour Force Survey data from 2006 to August 2026) found that youth (15 to 29) hiring rates have fallen in every occupation group [2]. Coding-intensive jobs—the closest group to ECE co-op roles—now have the lowest youth hiring rate of all groups measured at 1.9%, a drastic drop from a 15-year high of 5.8% in late 2021 [2].

  • 03 / Employer Workforce Plans [6]

    40% of employers plan automation cuts

    The drop comes mainly from employers hiring fewer young workers rather than laying them off, concentrated in jobs where AI automates tasks while experienced workers are not impacted by much [1]. Looking ahead, 40% of employers surveyed in the World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025 plan to reduce their workforce in areas where AI can automate tasks [6].

  • 01 / First-Year Co-op Experience

    When AI does everything a junior dev is hired to do

    This past summer I worked as a junior backend developer. During that internship, AI was capable of doing everything I did on the job, which hindered my learning experience since my employer preferred using AI as it produced working code much faster.

  • 02 / Conceptual vs. Delegated Coding [3]

    Passing coding to AI drops quiz scores below 40%

    In Shen and Tamkin’s randomized experiment with 52 developers learning Trio, participants who passed all coding to AI—or used AI to debug and verify code—averaged below 40% on the follow-up quiz [3]. Those who only asked conceptual questions and fixed their own errors scored 65% or higher (Table I) [3].

  • 03 / Upper-Year Capstone Students (A–D)

    Eroding foundational debugging skills

    Four upper-year Waterloo ECE students completing their capstone projects (Students A to D) reported that while AI sped up coding and syntax fixes on their work terms, it hindered their development of foundational debugging skills—the exact area where Shen and Tamkin found the largest comprehension gap [3]. They recommended modernizing the curriculum with AI coding CLIs, AI-based work architecture, and LLM integration.

Waterloo Leverage: 8,000+ Co-op Employers [10] SPE Rates Output, Not Retention [8]
  • 01 / The Illusion of a Strong Evaluation

    The SPE measures job performance—and AI inflates performance

    Since a co-op term is four months of junior work, a student could finish a term with an outstanding Student Performance Evaluation (SPE) [8] and still not build the engineering judgment the term was supposed to give them. The SPE will not catch this because it rates performance on the job, and AI directly increases novice output [4], [5].

  • 02 / Ethical & Engineering Safety Risk

    Who loses the most—and professional accountability

    Students who already have strong fundamentals can use AI to work faster without losing much, while students in their first or second work term lose the most. Furthermore, engineers are responsible for the work they approve: a graduate who cannot debug or explain code written by AI is a safety risk, not just a weaker hire.

  • 03 / Why Alternatives Fall Short

    Neither banning AI nor voluntary check-ins work

    Banning AI in design courses would protect short-term learning but goes against upper-year student recommendations and leaves graduates unprepared for industry. Leaving employer check-ins voluntary costs nothing, but Waterloo already recommends an informal mid-term evaluation [8] and that alone has not made it standard.

05 / Action Plan for Waterloo ECE Built on Existing Waterloo Processes [7], [8], [9], [11]
Section II • Recommendations

Three measures to protect student learning

ECE cannot control the North American job market, but it can control what students learn in their courses and co-op roles by upgrading three processes Waterloo already runs.

Zero New Bureaucratic Systems Required
A
Recommendation A • Curriculum Pilot

Teach and assess AI use in a second-year design course

Pilot a structured AI framework in one second-year ECE design course next term that permits students to use AI freely and completely while requiring them to document and justify why AI was appropriate for specific tasks. Allow repetitive tasks to be done completely by AI if the decision-making process and architecture are understood by the student.

Pair this with short no-AI debugging checks and sample oral defenses so students must independently explain and extend their work, and explicitly train students to ask conceptual questions instead of generating finished code [3].

Builds on: Mandatory Course Outline AI Policies [7] & ECE 198 Design Studio [11]
B
Recommendation B • WaterlooWorks Requirement

Add a required 20-minute midterm learning check-in to co-op terms

Partner with Waterloo’s Co-operative and Experiential Education office to require a brief midterm check-in (around week 6 or 7) that all employers on WaterlooWorks must agree to provide when hiring ECE students, assessing technical growth and design decisions on the job.

Provide employers with a one-page onboarding guide (Appendix B) to keep administrative burden low. With over 8,000 employers hiring through Waterloo co-op [10], the faculty has the leverage to make deep skill retention a condition of hiring from the ECE cohort.

Builds on: Existing Informal Mid-Term Evaluation Recommendation [8]
C
Recommendation C • Longitudinal Department Data

Track ECE-specific co-op outcomes and AI reliance

Have ECE faculty staff collect anonymous aggregated data at the end of each work term on placement rates, role responsibilities, and student-reported AI reliance using a few added questions in the Rate My Work Term survey students already complete [9], paired with aggregated check-in ratings from Recommendation B.

Because national labor statistics group occupations broadly [2] instead of isolating engineering disciplines, internal tracking gives ECE reliable evidence within a year on how entry-level work is shifting at Waterloo [1], [2].

Builds on: Anonymous Rate My Work Term Survey [9]

Appendix B • One-Page Onboarding Guide

Draft Employer Check-In Guide

Purpose: A 20-minute conversation around week six or seven of the work term that checks what the student has learned, in addition to what they have produced.

4 Questions for the Supervisor to Ask:
  1. 01What have you built or changed so far this term?
  2. 02Which part of that work could you rebuild or debug without AI tools?
  3. 03Explain one design decision you made and why you chose it.
  4. 04What skill will you focus on for the rest of the term?
Interactive WaterlooWorks Recording Preview (Select a Rating):
On track: The supervisor selects one rating in WaterlooWorks and may add one comment. The rating is shared with the student and used only in aggregate by ECE.
